GEO 112: REMOTE SENSING AND GEOGRAPHIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS (GIS)

Course description

This unit introduces the scientific principles, technologies, methods and applications of Remote Sensing and Geographic Information Systems. It examines how geographical information is collected, processed, stored, analysed and presented to understand spatial patterns and processes on the Earth's surface.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, a student should be able to:

  • Define Remote Sensing and GIS.
  • Explain the principles of electromagnetic radiation.
  • Describe the components and operation of remote sensing systems.
  • Differentiate between active and passive remote sensing.
  • Explain satellite imagery and image interpretation.
  • Understand spatial, spectral, temporal and radiometric resolution.
  • Identify GIS data types, components and applications.
  • Perform basic spatial analysis and interpret GIS outputs.
  • Apply Remote Sensing and GIS to environmental and development problems.
TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO REMOTE SENSING

1.1 Meaning of Remote Sensing

Remote sensing is the scientific process of collecting information about objects, phenomena or areas on the Earth's surface without making direct physical contact with them. It involves detecting and measuring energy reflected or emitted by objects using sensors mounted on satellites, aircraft, drones or other platforms.

For example, a satellite orbiting the Earth can collect information about forests, rivers, agricultural fields, settlements and oceans without a researcher physically visiting those locations.

Remote sensing enables geographers to observe large geographical areas, monitor environmental changes and study places that may be difficult or dangerous to access.

1.2 Historical development of Remote Sensing

Remote sensing developed through several technological stages:

  1. Early aerial photography: Cameras mounted on balloons and aircraft were used to capture images of the Earth's surface.
  2. Military applications: Aerial photographs became important for reconnaissance, surveillance and military mapping.
  3. Satellite technology: The development of artificial satellites made it possible to observe large areas of the Earth repeatedly.
  4. Digital remote sensing: Electronic sensors began recording numerical information that could be processed using computers.
  5. Modern remote sensing: Satellites, drones, radar, LiDAR, artificial intelligence and cloud computing have improved the collection and analysis of geographical information.

1.3 Main components of a remote sensing system

  1. Energy source: Provides electromagnetic energy. The Sun is the main source for many passive remote sensing systems.
  2. Atmosphere: The medium through which radiation travels. It can absorb and scatter electromagnetic energy.
  3. Target: The geographical object being studied, such as vegetation, water, soil or buildings.
  4. Sensor: An instrument that detects and records energy from the target.
  5. Platform: The vehicle carrying the sensor, such as a satellite, aircraft or drone.
  6. Data transmission: Recorded information is transmitted to receiving stations.
  7. Data processing: Raw data are corrected, enhanced and converted into useful images or information.
  8. Interpretation and application: Information is analysed and used to solve geographical problems.

1.4 Types of Remote Sensing

A. Passive Remote Sensing

Passive remote sensing uses naturally available energy reflected or emitted by objects. Most optical satellite imagery relies on sunlight reflected from the Earth's surface.

Examples include:

  • Satellite cameras recording reflected sunlight.
  • Thermal sensors detecting naturally emitted heat.
  • Optical sensors recording vegetation and water features.

B. Active Remote Sensing

Active remote sensing involves a sensor transmitting its own energy towards a target and recording the energy reflected or scattered back to the sensor.

Examples include:

  • RADAR: Uses radio or microwave energy to study the Earth's surface.
  • LiDAR: Uses laser pulses to measure distances and create detailed elevation information.

1.5 Remote Sensing Platforms

A platform is the vehicle or structure on which a remote sensing instrument is mounted.

  • Ground-based platforms: Instruments operated from the Earth's surface, often for scientific measurements and calibration.
  • Airborne platforms: Aircraft, helicopters and drones carrying cameras and sensors.
  • Spaceborne platforms: Satellites orbiting the Earth and collecting geographical information over large areas.

1.6 Applications of Remote Sensing

  • Agriculture: Monitoring crop health, identifying drought stress and estimating cultivated areas.
  • Forestry: Mapping forest cover, deforestation and forest degradation.
  • Hydrology: Monitoring rivers, lakes, wetlands and flood extent.
  • Urban planning: Monitoring urban expansion and changes in settlement patterns.
  • Geology: Identifying geological structures and studying rock formations.
  • Disaster management: Mapping floods, fires, landslides and drought impacts.
  • Climate studies: Monitoring land surface temperature, snow cover and other environmental indicators.

1.7 Advantages of Remote Sensing

  1. Covers extensive geographical areas within a relatively short period.
  2. Provides repeated observations for monitoring environmental changes.
  3. Can collect information from inaccessible or dangerous locations.
  4. Provides information in different electromagnetic wavelengths.
  5. Supports environmental planning and natural resource management.
  6. Digital imagery can be integrated with GIS for further analysis.

1.8 Limitations of Remote Sensing

  1. High-resolution imagery and specialised equipment can be expensive.
  2. Optical sensors may be affected by clouds, haze and atmospheric conditions.
  3. Images require specialised knowledge and software for interpretation.
  4. Different surface materials may have similar spectral characteristics.
  5. Errors can occur during image acquisition and processing.
  6. Field verification may still be necessary to confirm what is observed in an image.
TOPIC 2: ELECTROMAGNETIC RADIATION AND THE ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM

2.1 Meaning of Electromagnetic Radiation

Electromagnetic radiation is energy that travels through space in the form of interacting electric and magnetic waves. It is fundamental to remote sensing because sensors detect electromagnetic energy reflected, transmitted or emitted by objects.

The Sun provides much of the energy used by passive optical remote sensing systems. The Earth also emits energy, particularly in the thermal infrared region.

2.2 Characteristics of Electromagnetic Waves

  • Wavelength: The distance between two successive wave peaks.
  • Frequency: The number of complete wave cycles passing a point per second.
  • Amplitude: The maximum displacement of a wave from its normal position.
  • Energy: The amount of energy carried by electromagnetic radiation.

The relationship between wavelength, frequency and the speed of light is:

c = λf

  • c = speed of light.
  • λ = wavelength.
  • f = frequency.

The energy of a photon is calculated using:

E = hf

Where E is energy, h is Planck's constant and f is frequency.

The electromagnetic spectrum refers to the complete range of electromagnetic radiation, arranged according to wavelength or frequency.

The main regions include:

  1. Radio waves: Used in communication and some remote sensing applications.
  2. Microwaves: Important in radar remote sensing because they can penetrate clouds and are sensitive to surface moisture and structure.
  3. Infrared: Useful for studying vegetation, moisture and surface temperature.
  4. Visible light: The part of the spectrum detectable by the human eye, consisting of red, green and blue light.
  5. Ultraviolet: Used in specialised environmental and atmospheric studies.
  6. X-rays and gamma rays: High-energy radiation with specialised scientific applications.

2.4 Interaction of Radiation with the Atmosphere

As electromagnetic radiation passes through the atmosphere, it interacts with gases, dust particles, aerosols and clouds.

Three important processes occur:

  • Absorption: Atmospheric gases absorb energy at particular wavelengths, reducing the amount that reaches the sensor.
  • Scattering: Radiation is redirected by particles and molecules in the atmosphere.
  • Transmission: Radiation passes through the atmosphere without being absorbed or scattered significantly.

An atmospheric window is a wavelength region in which radiation can pass through the atmosphere relatively easily. Remote sensing instruments are designed to use suitable atmospheric windows.

2.5 Interaction of Radiation with Earth's Surface

When electromagnetic radiation reaches an object, it may undergo three main processes:

  • Reflection: Energy is redirected from the surface.
  • Absorption: Energy is taken in by the object.
  • Transmission: Energy passes through the object.

2.6 Spectral Signatures

A spectral signature is the characteristic pattern of reflected or emitted electromagnetic energy from an object across different wavelengths.

Different geographical materials have different spectral responses. For example:

  • Healthy green vegetation strongly reflects near-infrared radiation.
  • Water generally absorbs much of the near-infrared energy and therefore often appears dark in near-infrared images.
  • Dry bare soil and wet soil may have different reflectance characteristics.
  • Built-up surfaces have varying spectral responses depending on their construction materials.

Spectral signatures help geographers distinguish vegetation, water, soil, rocks and urban surfaces in satellite imagery.

2.7 Importance of Electromagnetic Radiation in Remote Sensing

  1. It provides the energy detected by remote sensing instruments.
  2. It makes it possible to distinguish different surface materials.
  3. It supports the identification of vegetation conditions.
  4. It allows measurement of thermal conditions.
  5. It supports land-cover classification and environmental monitoring.
TOPIC 3: SATELLITES, SENSORS AND RESOLUTION

3.1 Meaning of a Satellite

A satellite is an object that revolves around another, larger object. In remote sensing, artificial Earth-observation satellites carry sensors that collect information about the Earth's surface and atmosphere.

3.2 Types of Earth Observation Orbits

  • Polar orbit: A satellite travels approximately from north to south over the poles as the Earth rotates beneath it. It can progressively observe different parts of the Earth's surface.
  • Sun-synchronous orbit: A near-polar orbit designed so that the satellite passes over a location at approximately the same local solar time on successive observations.
  • Geostationary orbit: A satellite orbits above the equator at a rate matching the Earth's rotation, appearing stationary over a particular location. It is widely used for weather observation.

3.3 Meaning of a Sensor

A sensor is an instrument that detects and records electromagnetic energy. It may be designed to measure visible light, infrared radiation, microwave energy or other wavelength regions.

3.4 Characteristics of Remote Sensing Sensors

Sensor performance is commonly described using four types of resolution.

A. Spatial Resolution

Spatial resolution refers to the ground area represented by an individual pixel in a digital image. For example, a 10-metre spatial resolution means that a pixel represents approximately 10 m × 10 m on the ground in the image's nominal ground sampling distance.

Higher spatial resolution generally allows smaller features to be distinguished.

B. Spectral Resolution

Spectral resolution refers to the ability of a sensor to distinguish different wavelength regions. Sensors with many narrow spectral bands can detect more detailed differences in the spectral characteristics of surface materials.

C. Radiometric Resolution

Radiometric resolution refers to the ability of a sensor to distinguish small differences in recorded energy intensity. It is often expressed in bits.

For example, an 8-bit image can represent 256 possible digital values, ranging from 0 to 255.

D. Temporal Resolution

Temporal resolution refers to how frequently a sensor obtains observations of the same geographical area.

High temporal resolution is useful when studying rapidly changing phenomena such as floods, vegetation conditions and weather systems.

3.5 Examples of Earth Observation Satellites

  • Landsat: Used for monitoring land cover, agriculture, forests, water bodies and environmental changes.
  • Sentinel-1: Uses radar technology for observing the Earth's surface.
  • Sentinel-2: Provides multispectral imagery useful for vegetation, land cover and water monitoring.
  • MODIS: Used for large-scale environmental and climate observations.
  • GOES: Geostationary weather satellites used for atmospheric monitoring.

3.6 Image Resolution and Pixel Size

A pixel is the smallest individual picture element in a digital raster image. Each pixel stores a numerical value representing the measurement recorded for the corresponding ground area.

For example, a 30 m × 30 m pixel represents:

Area = 30 × 30 = 900 m².

If 100 pixels represent a particular land-cover class, their nominal combined area is:

100 × 900 = 90,000 m² = 9 hectares.

Important: Spatial resolution, image scale and the actual ability to identify an object are related but are not identical. A smaller pixel does not automatically guarantee that every small object will be clearly identifiable.
TOPIC 4: SATELLITE IMAGERY AND IMAGE INTERPRETATION

4.1 Meaning of Satellite Imagery

Satellite imagery refers to digital or photographic representations of the Earth's surface produced from measurements collected by sensors on satellites.

These images provide information about the distribution and characteristics of geographical features.

4.2 Types of Satellite Images

  • Panchromatic images: Images recorded in a single broad wavelength band, usually displayed in shades of grey.
  • Multispectral images: Images containing several wavelength bands, including visible and infrared bands.
  • Hyperspectral images: Images containing many narrow and closely spaced spectral bands.
  • Thermal images: Images produced from measurements of thermal infrared radiation.
  • Radar images: Images generated from microwave energy transmitted and received by active radar systems.

4.3 Image Interpretation

Image interpretation is the process of identifying, describing and analysing objects and geographical features appearing in aerial photographs or satellite imagery.

4.4 Elements of Visual Image Interpretation

  1. Tone and colour: The brightness or colour of an object. Different surfaces reflect different amounts of energy.
  2. Shape: The general form or outline of an object. Roads, buildings and rivers may have recognisable shapes.
  3. Size: The dimensions of a feature in relation to other objects and the image scale.
  4. Texture: The frequency of tonal changes in an image. Forests may appear rougher than uniform grassland.
  5. Pattern: The spatial arrangement or repetition of features. Agricultural fields may form regular patterns.
  6. Shadow: Dark areas produced when objects block incoming radiation. Shadows can help estimate the height and shape of features.
  7. Site: The geographical location or topographic position of an object.
  8. Association: The relationship between a feature and nearby objects. For example, a railway station may be associated with railway tracks.

4.5 False Colour Composite

A false colour composite is an image produced by assigning spectral bands to display colours that do not necessarily match the natural colours perceived by the human eye.

For example, when near-infrared is assigned to the red display channel, healthy vegetation often appears bright red because it strongly reflects near-infrared energy.

4.6 Digital Image Processing

Digital image processing involves computer-based operations performed on satellite imagery to improve its quality or extract useful information.

Common operations include:

  • Geometric correction: Correcting positional and geometric distortions.
  • Radiometric correction: Correcting sensor-related and illumination-related differences in recorded values.
  • Atmospheric correction: Reducing the influence of atmospheric effects on image measurements.
  • Image enhancement: Improving image appearance to make features easier to identify.
  • Image classification: Assigning image pixels to categories such as water, vegetation, soil and built-up land.
  • Change detection: Comparing images from different dates to identify changes on the Earth's surface.

4.7 Land Use and Land Cover

Land cover describes the physical materials covering the Earth's surface, such as forests, water, bare soil and grassland.

Land use describes the human purpose or activity associated with a particular area, such as residential development, farming, industry and recreation.

Remote sensing supports the mapping of both land use and land cover, although land use often requires additional contextual information.

4.8 Applications in Kenya

  • Monitoring forest cover in the Mau Forest Complex.
  • Monitoring agricultural conditions in the Rift Valley.
  • Mapping urban expansion in Nairobi and Kisumu.
  • Monitoring wetlands around Lake Victoria.
  • Assessing drought conditions in arid and semi-arid counties.
  • Mapping flood extent in river basins.
TOPIC 5: INTRODUCTION TO GEOGRAPHIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS (GIS)

5.1 Meaning of GIS

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is an organised system of computer hardware, software, geographical data, people and procedures used to collect, store, manage, manipulate, analyse and display information associated with specific locations on the Earth's surface.

GIS links geographical location with descriptive information. This enables users to understand where features are located, what characteristics they possess and how they relate spatially to one another.

For example, a GIS database can store the locations of hospitals together with information about their names, bed capacities, services and accessibility.

5.2 History and Development of GIS

GIS developed from early mapping and computer-based spatial analysis. Its development was influenced by advances in computer technology, cartography, surveying, remote sensing and geography.

  • Traditional paper maps provided the foundation for spatial information.
  • Computer mapping improved the production and storage of maps.
  • Digital databases enabled geographical data to be stored and retrieved more efficiently.
  • Modern GIS integrates satellite imagery, GPS, mobile devices, internet mapping and spatial analysis.

5.3 Components of GIS

A functional GIS consists of five major components:

1. Hardware

Physical computer equipment used to operate GIS, including computers, servers, storage devices, scanners, GPS receivers and printers.

2. Software

Computer programmes used to create, edit, analyse and display spatial information.

Examples include QGIS, ArcGIS Pro and GRASS GIS.

3. Data

Geographical information is the foundation of any GIS operation. It includes spatial data and attribute data.

4. People

GIS professionals, geographers, planners, surveyors, researchers and decision-makers who operate and interpret GIS information.

5. Methods and Procedures

Rules, techniques and workflows followed when collecting, processing, analysing and presenting geographical information.

5.4 Functions of GIS

  1. Data capture: Collecting geographical information through GPS, surveying, digitisation, remote sensing and other methods.
  2. Data storage: Organising geographical information in digital databases.
  3. Data management: Editing, updating and maintaining spatial datasets.
  4. Data manipulation: Changing or transforming geographical data into a suitable format.
  5. Spatial analysis: Investigating geographical relationships, patterns and processes.
  6. Data visualisation: Presenting results using maps, graphs and other visual outputs.

5.5 Applications of GIS

  • Urban planning: Mapping settlements, roads, land use and public facilities.
  • Agriculture: Identifying suitable farming areas and monitoring crop conditions.
  • Health: Mapping disease distribution and the accessibility of healthcare facilities.

  • Disaster management: Identifying hazard-prone areas and supporting emergency response.
  • Population studies: Mapping population density and settlement distribution.
  • Land administration: Mapping property boundaries and land parcels.
TOPIC 6: GIS DATA TYPES AND DATA STRUCTURES

6.1 Meaning of Spatial Data

Spatial data refers to information describing the geographical location, shape, size and spatial relationships of features on the Earth's surface.

6.2 Meaning of Attribute Data

Attribute data refers to descriptive information associated with geographical features.

For example, a point representing a school may have attributes such as:

  • School name.
  • School type.
  • Number of students.
  • Number of teachers.
  • Ownership.

6.3 Vector Data

Vector data represents geographical features using points, lines and polygons.

A. Points

Points represent geographical locations that are too small to be represented meaningfully by their actual area at the selected map scale.

Examples: boreholes, schools, hospitals, bus stops and individual trees.

B. Lines

Lines represent linear geographical features.

Examples: roads, rivers, pipelines, railways and electricity transmission lines.

C. Polygons

Polygons represent enclosed areas with defined boundaries.

Examples: counties, forests, lakes, farms and administrative boundaries.

6.4 Raster Data

Raster data represents geographical information as a grid of cells or pixels. Each cell contains a value describing the characteristic of the corresponding geographical area.

Raster data is useful for representing continuous geographical phenomena such as elevation, rainfall, temperature and satellite imagery.

TOPIC 7: COORDINATE SYSTEMS, DATUMS AND MAP PROJECTIONS

7.1 Geographic Coordinates

A geographic coordinate system identifies locations on the Earth's surface using angular measurements of latitude and longitude.

  • Latitude: Angular distance north or south of the Equator.
  • Longitude: Angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian.

7.2 Geographic Coordinate System (GCS)

A GCS is a reference system that uses latitude and longitude to identify positions on a three-dimensional model of the Earth. It depends on a datum and an angular coordinate framework.

7.3 Projected Coordinate System (PCS)

A projected coordinate system represents the Earth's curved surface on a flat two-dimensional plane using a map projection. Positions are usually expressed in linear units such as metres.

7.4 Map Projections

A map projection is a mathematical method of transforming locations on the curved Earth onto a flat map.

Common projection families include:

  • Cylindrical projections: Project the Earth's surface onto a cylinder.
  • Conical projections: Project the Earth's surface onto a cone.
  • Azimuthal projections: Project the Earth's surface onto a flat plane.

Every map projection introduces some distortion in shape, area, distance or direction. The appropriate projection depends on the purpose and geographical extent of the map.

7.5 Coordinate Reference Systems in GIS

A Coordinate Reference System (CRS) defines how spatial coordinates relate to actual positions on the Earth.

Examples include:

  • WGS 84 geographic coordinates.
  • UTM projected coordinate systems.
  • National and regional projected coordinate systems.
Practical importance: Layers with incompatible coordinate reference systems may appear in different positions when displayed together. Reprojection and appropriate CRS selection help align spatial datasets.

7.6 Importance of Coordinate Systems

  1. Enable accurate geographical positioning.
  2. Support measurement of distances and areas.
  3. Allow integration of different spatial datasets.
  4. Support navigation and surveying.
  5. Improve the accuracy of spatial analysis and map production.
TOPIC 8: SPATIAL ANALYSIS IN GIS

8.1 Meaning of Spatial Analysis

Spatial analysis refers to the application of mathematical, statistical and computational techniques to geographical data to identify spatial patterns, relationships and processes.

8.2 Major GIS Spatial Analysis Techniques

1. Buffer Analysis

Buffer analysis creates a zone around a geographical feature at a specified distance.

Example: Creating a 500-metre buffer around schools to study nearby residential development.

2. Overlay Analysis

Overlay analysis combines two or more geographical layers to identify relationships between them.

Example: Combining soil, slope and rainfall maps to identify suitable agricultural areas.

3. Network Analysis

Network analysis studies movement and connectivity through networks such as roads, railways and pipelines.

Example: Finding the shortest route between a village and the nearest hospital.

4. Proximity Analysis

Proximity analysis determines how close geographical features are to one another.

Example: Identifying households located within a certain distance of a water source.

5. Interpolation

Interpolation estimates unknown values at locations using known observations from surrounding locations.

Example: Estimating rainfall distribution between weather stations.

6. Terrain Analysis

Terrain analysis uses elevation information to study land surface characteristics.

Examples include slope, aspect, contours and hillshade.

7. Spatial Query

A spatial query selects features according to their location or spatial relationship with other features.

Example: Selecting all schools located inside a particular county.

8.3 Importance of Spatial Analysis

  • Helps identify geographical patterns.
  • Supports evidence-based planning.
  • Improves resource allocation.
  • Helps locate suitable sites for development projects.
  • Supports disaster risk assessment.
  • Improves environmental monitoring.

8.4 Practical Example: Selecting a Suitable Hospital Site

Suppose a county government wants to establish a new hospital.

GIS can help by:

  1. Mapping existing hospitals.
  2. Mapping roads and settlements.
  3. Identifying densely populated areas with limited access to healthcare.
  4. Excluding unsuitable locations such as flood-prone land.
  5. Combining the geographical layers through overlay analysis.
  6. Identifying and ranking suitable locations.

TOPIC 11: CHALLENGES, ETHICS AND FUTURE OF GIS AND REMOTE SENSING

11.1 Technical Challenges

  • Inaccurate or incomplete geographical datasets.
  • High costs of some satellite imagery and equipment.
  • Limited computing infrastructure.
  • Complex data processing requirements.
  • Incompatibility between datasets and coordinate systems.

11.2 Institutional and Human Challenges

  • Shortage of trained GIS professionals.
  • Limited funding for geographical projects.
  • Inadequate access to updated spatial information.
  • Limited awareness of GIS applications among decision-makers.

11.3 Ethical Issues

  • Protection of personal and sensitive location information.
  • Responsible collection and use of geographical data.
  • Respect for privacy and property rights.
  • Transparency in spatial analysis and map presentation.
  • Avoiding misleading maps and inaccurate interpretations.

11.4 Future Developments

The future of GIS and Remote Sensing is influenced by several technologies:

  • Artificial intelligence and machine learning.
  • Unmanned aerial vehicles and drones.
  • Real-time satellite monitoring.
  • Cloud-based GIS platforms.
  • Three-dimensional mapping.
  • High-resolution Earth observation.
  • Integration of GIS with mobile technologies and the Internet of Things.

1. Define Remote Sensing and explain five of its applications.

Answer: Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about objects or areas without physical contact, through the detection of reflected or emitted energy.

Applications include agriculture, forestry, urban planning, disaster management and environmental monitoring.

2. Differentiate between active and passive remote sensing.

Answer: Passive remote sensing detects naturally available reflected or emitted energy, whereas active remote sensing transmits its own energy and measures the returned signal. Examples are optical satellite imagery for passive sensing and RADAR for active sensing.

3. Explain the four types of remote sensing resolution.

Answer:

  • Spatial: Ground area represented by a pixel.
  • Spectral: Ability to distinguish wavelength bands.
  • Radiometric: Ability to distinguish differences in energy intensity.
  • Temporal: Frequency of repeat observations.
4. Explain the meaning of spectral signature and its importance.

Answer: A spectral signature is the characteristic pattern of electromagnetic energy reflected or emitted by an object across different wavelengths. It helps identify and classify vegetation, water, soil and other surface materials.

5. Explain the five major components of GIS.

Answer: Hardware, software, data, people and methods or procedures. All five work together to collect, manage, analyse and present geographical information.

6. Differentiate between raster and vector data.

Answer: Raster data represents geographical information through a grid of cells, while vector data represents features using points, lines and polygons.

7. Explain five applications of GIS in Kenya.

Answer: Urban planning, agricultural planning, disease mapping, transport planning and environmental conservation.

8. Discuss the relationship between GIS and Remote Sensing.

Answer: Remote sensing collects information about the Earth's surface using sensors, while GIS stores, integrates and analyses the resulting information together with other spatial datasets. They complement one another in geographical research and planning.

9. Explain five elements of image interpretation.

Answer: Tone or colour, shape, size, texture, pattern, shadow, site and association. Any five well-explained elements earn credit.

10. Explain how GIS can support disaster management.

Answer: GIS can map hazard-prone areas, identify vulnerable populations, analyse evacuation routes, locate emergency services and support the allocation of relief resources.

12. Discuss the advantages and limitations of Remote Sensing.

Answer: Advantages include large-area coverage, repeated observation, access to inaccessible places and environmental monitoring. Limitations include cost, atmospheric interference, processing complexity and the need for field verification.

COURSE INTRODUCTION

Cartography is an important branch of Geography concerned with the science, art, technology and communication of geographical information through maps. It involves collecting, selecting, organising, representing, designing, producing and interpreting maps.

Maps are essential geographical tools because they communicate information about the Earth's surface, including the location, distribution, movement and relationships of natural and human phenomena.

  • Calculate and interpret map scales.
  • Explain coordinate systems and map projections.

  • Construct and interpret thematic maps.

TOPIC 1: INTRODUCTION TO CARTOGRAPHY

1.1 Definition of Cartography

Cartography is the discipline concerned with the study and practice of making, designing, producing and interpreting maps. It combines geographical knowledge, scientific measurement, artistic design and communication techniques to represent spatial information.

In simple terms, cartography is the science and art of making maps.

A cartographer is a person who specialises in the preparation, design and production of maps.

1.2 Meaning of a Map

A map is a scaled, simplified and symbolic representation of selected geographical features or phenomena on the Earth's surface, or another spatial environment, presented on a flat surface or digital display.

A map does not normally represent every object in the real world. Instead, the map-maker selects the information that is important for the map's intended purpose.

For example, a road map emphasises roads, settlements and transport connections, while a geological map emphasises rock formations, geological structures and boundaries.

1.3 Nature of Cartography

Cartography has several important characteristics:

  1. Scientific: It involves measurement, mathematical calculations, surveying, coordinate systems and geographical accuracy.
  2. Artistic: It involves visual design, colour selection, symbols, lettering and arrangement of map elements.
  3. Technological: It uses computers, GIS software, satellite imagery, GPS and digital printing technologies.
  4. Communicative: It transfers geographical information from the map-maker to the map reader.
  5. Selective: It simplifies reality by including only information relevant to the purpose of a map.

1.4 Scope of Cartography

The scope of cartography extends beyond drawing maps. It includes:

  • Map design and layout.
  • Map compilation and production.
  • Map projections and coordinate systems.
  • Cartographic symbols and lettering.
  • Topographic mapping.
  • Thematic mapping.
  • Map interpretation and analysis.
  • Digital and web cartography.
  • Cartographic communication and visualisation.

1.5 History and Development of Cartography

Cartography has developed from simple drawings of local environments into sophisticated digital systems capable of displaying geographical information in real time.

A. Ancient cartography

Early societies produced maps to represent settlements, agricultural land, trade routes, rivers and territories. Ancient maps were often drawn on clay tablets, stone, papyrus and other available materials.

B. Greek contributions

Greek scholars contributed significantly to geographical knowledge, including ideas about the Earth's shape, geographical coordinates and mathematical approaches to mapping.

Important contributors included:

  • Anaximander: Associated with early Greek attempts to represent the known world.
  • Eratosthenes: Made an important calculation of the Earth's circumference.
  • Ptolemy: Contributed to mathematical geography and the use of latitude and longitude in mapping.

C. Medieval cartography

During the medieval period, maps were produced for religious, administrative, commercial and navigational purposes. Different regions developed distinctive cartographic traditions.

D. Renaissance cartography

Exploration, maritime trade, printing and improvements in geographical measurement encouraged the development of more accurate maps. Navigation charts became increasingly important.

E. Modern cartography

Scientific surveying, aerial photography, photogrammetry, improved instruments and mathematical techniques made maps more accurate.

F. Digital cartography

Modern cartography uses GIS, remote sensing, GPS, computers, web mapping and mobile technologies. Maps can now be updated, analysed and shared electronically.

  • Flat maps introduce distortions because the Earth is curved.

  • Poor map design can mislead the map reader.
  • Some maps may contain errors in measurement, data or interpretation.
TOPIC 2: MAPS, THEIR CHARACTERISTICS AND CLASSIFICATION

2.1 Characteristics of Maps

Most conventional maps have the following characteristics:

  1. Scale: A map represents geographical distances at a reduced or enlarged ratio.
  2. Symbols: Features are represented using conventional signs and symbols.
  3. Projection: A mathematical method is used to represent the curved Earth on a flat surface.
  4. Orientation: Maps normally indicate direction, commonly with north at the top.
  5. Generalisation: Unnecessary details are simplified or omitted.
  6. Selection: Features are included according to the purpose of the map.
  7. Title: The title indicates the subject or geographical area represented.

2.2 Classification According to Scale

A. Large-scale maps

These maps represent relatively small geographical areas with considerable detail.

Examples include cadastral maps, town plans and detailed engineering maps.

Example scale: 1:5,000 or 1:10,000.

B. Medium-scale maps

These represent intermediate geographical areas and provide a moderate amount of detail.

Example scale: 1:50,000 or 1:100,000.

C. Small-scale maps

These represent large geographical areas with relatively less detail.

Examples include national, continental and world maps.

Example scale: 1:1,000,000.

Remember: A large-scale map has a smaller denominator and shows more detail over a smaller area. A small-scale map has a larger denominator and shows less detail over a larger area.

2.3 Classification According to Purpose

  • General reference maps: Show a variety of geographical features, including settlements, roads, rivers and boundaries.
  • Thematic maps: Focus on a particular geographical subject, such as rainfall, population or soil distribution.
  • Topographic maps: Show both natural and human-made features, including elevation and relief.
  • Cadastral maps: Show land parcels, property boundaries and ownership-related information.
  • Navigation maps: Support movement by land, air or sea.
  • Geological maps: Represent rock types, geological structures and formations.
  • Political maps: Show administrative and political boundaries.
  • Climate maps: Represent climatic characteristics such as rainfall and temperature.
  • Economic maps: Show economic activities, industries and resource distribution.
  • Tourist maps: Identify tourist attractions, accommodation and transport routes.

2.4 General Reference Maps

General reference maps provide information about several geographical features in one representation.

They may include:

  • Roads and railways.
  • Rivers and lakes.
  • Settlements and administrative boundaries.
  • Mountains and other landforms.
  • Major public facilities.

2.5 Thematic Maps

A thematic map is designed to communicate the spatial distribution or characteristics of a particular subject.

Examples include:

  • Population density in Kenyan counties.
  • Rainfall distribution in Kenya.
  • Forest cover in the Mau region.
  • Unemployment distribution.
  • Crop production by county.

2.6 Topographic Maps

A topographic map represents the natural and human-made features of an area and uses contour lines or other elevation methods to show the shape and height of the land.

Its features may include:

  • Contour lines and spot heights.
  • Rivers and lakes.
  • Roads and railways.
  • Vegetation and woodland.
  • Settlements and buildings.
  • Administrative boundaries.

2.7 Special-purpose Maps

These are maps prepared for specific professional or research needs. Examples include soil maps, military maps, engineering maps, navigation charts and flood-risk maps.

TOPIC 4: MAP SCALE AND SCALE CALCULATIONS

4.1 Meaning of Map Scale

Map scale is the relationship between a distance measured on a map and the corresponding horizontal distance on the ground.

Maps are usually smaller than the real geographical areas they represent. Scale enables distances on the map to be converted into actual ground distances.

4.2 Types of Map Scale

A. Statement or verbal scale

Expresses scale using words.

Example: 1 centimetre represents 1 kilometre on the ground.

B. Representative Fraction (RF)

Expresses scale as a ratio between map distance and ground distance in the same units.

Example: 1:50,000 means one unit on the map represents 50,000 of the same units on the ground.

C. Linear or graphic scale

Represents scale using a graduated line divided into sections representing ground distances.

4.3 Understanding Representative Fraction

Consider a map with a scale of 1:100,000.

This means:

  • 1 cm on the map represents 100,000 cm on the ground.
  • 100,000 cm = 1,000 metres.
  • 1,000 metres = 1 kilometre.

Therefore, 1 cm on this map represents 1 km on the ground.

4.4 Formula for Calculating Map Scale

RF = Map distance ÷ Ground distance

Both distances must be converted into the same units before calculating the ratio.

4.5 Calculating Ground Distance

Ground distance = Map distance × Scale denominator

Example:

A map has a scale of 1:50,000. The distance between two towns on the map is 4 cm. Calculate the actual ground distance.

Solution:

Ground distance = 4 × 50,000

= 200,000 cm

= 2,000 metres

= 2 kilometres.

4.6 Calculating Map Distance

Map distance = Ground distance ÷ Scale denominator

Example:

The actual distance between two locations is 5 km. A map has a scale of 1:100,000. Calculate the map distance.

Convert 5 km into centimetres:

5 km = 500,000 cm.

Map distance = 500,000 ÷ 100,000

= 5 cm.

4.7 Calculating RF

A road measures 6 cm on a map and represents 3 km on the ground. Determine the RF.

Convert 3 km to centimetres:

3 km = 300,000 cm.

RF = 6 ÷ 300,000

= 1/50,000.

Answer: 1:50,000.

4.8 Measuring Curved Distances

Curved geographical features such as rivers and winding roads cannot be measured accurately using a straight ruler alone.

Common methods include:

  • Using a piece of thread along the curved route and measuring its length.
  • Using a divider or pair of compasses to measure short sections.
  • Using a digital GIS measuring tool.

4.9 Enlargement and Reduction of Maps

Map enlargement increases the physical size of a map, while map reduction decreases its physical size.

If a map is enlarged or reduced, its representative fraction changes. The scale statement must therefore be recalculated.

TOPIC 5: THE EARTH, COORDINATES AND LOCATION

5.1 Shape of the Earth

The Earth is not a perfect sphere. It is approximately an oblate spheroid, meaning it is slightly flattened at the poles and bulges around the Equator. Its actual shape is more accurately described by the geoid, an irregular surface related to the Earth's gravity field.

5.2 Importance of the Earth's Shape in Cartography

  • Determines how geographical positions are calculated.
  • Influences the selection of map projections.
  • Affects distance and area calculations.
  • Provides the basis for coordinate systems and geodetic measurements.

5.3 Latitude

Latitude is the angular distance of a location north or south of the Equator, measured in degrees.

  • Equator: 0° latitude.
  • North Pole: 90° N.
  • South Pole: 90° S.

5.4 Longitude

Longitude is the angular distance of a location east or west of the Prime Meridian, measured in degrees.

  • Prime Meridian: 0° longitude.
  • Eastern Hemisphere: east of the Prime Meridian.
  • Western Hemisphere: west of the Prime Meridian.

5.5 Important Lines of Latitude

  • Equator: 0°.
  • Tropic of Cancer: approximately 23.5° N.
  • Tropic of Capricorn: approximately 23.5° S.
  • Arctic Circle: approximately 66.5° N.
  • Antarctic Circle: approximately 66.5° S.

5.6 Geographic Coordinate System

A geographic coordinate system uses latitude and longitude to identify positions on the Earth's surface. It is based on a datum and a mathematical model of the Earth.

5.7 Grid References

Grid references use a system of intersecting lines to identify positions on a map.

In a conventional eastings-and-northings grid:

  • Eastings are read first.
  • Northings are read second.

This is commonly remembered as along the corridor, then up the stairs.

5.8 Four-Figure and Six-Figure Grid References

A four-figure grid reference identifies a grid square. A six-figure grid reference gives a more precise position within that square by dividing it into tenths in both directions.

5.9 Importance of Coordinates

  1. Identifying precise locations.
  2. Navigation and surveying.
  3. Preparing accurate maps.
  4. Integrating geographical datasets.
  5. Supporting GIS and GPS applications.
TOPIC 6: MAP PROJECTIONS

6.1 Meaning of Map Projection

A map projection is a mathematical method used to transform the curved surface of the Earth or a globe onto a flat map.

Because the Earth is curved, it is impossible to flatten its entire surface onto a plane without introducing some form of distortion.

6.2 Why Map Projections Are Necessary

  • To represent the Earth on flat paper or screens.
  • To prepare maps for navigation and planning.
  • To calculate and display geographical positions.
  • To represent large geographical areas.
  • To support geographical analysis and communication.

6.3 Distortions in Map Projections

Map projections may distort four main properties:

A. Shape

Some projections preserve local angles and shapes, while others distort them.

B. Area

Some projections preserve relative area, while others make certain regions appear larger or smaller than their actual relative sizes.

C. Distance

Distances may be preserved accurately only along particular lines or from particular points, depending on the projection.

D. Direction

Directions may be preserved from selected points or along particular lines, but not necessarily everywhere on a map.

6.4 Classification According to Projection Surface

1. Cylindrical projection

The Earth is projected onto a cylinder. The cylinder is commonly imagined touching the globe along the Equator or another selected line.

Example: Mercator projection.

It is useful for certain navigation purposes because it preserves local angles, but it greatly exaggerates the areas of regions near the poles.

2. Conical projection

The Earth is projected onto a cone placed over the globe. It is often suitable for mid-latitude regions with an east-west orientation.

3. Azimuthal or planar projection

The Earth's surface is projected onto a flat plane touching or intersecting the globe at a selected point or region.

It is particularly useful for polar mapping and certain distance or direction applications.

6.5 Classification According to Properties Preserved

  • Conformal projections: Preserve local angles and shapes at sufficiently small scales, but distort area.
  • Equal-area projections: Preserve relative areas but may distort shapes.
  • Equidistant projections: Preserve selected distances from particular points or along particular lines.
  • True-direction projections: Preserve direction from selected points or along particular lines.

6.7 Mercator Projection

The Mercator projection is a cylindrical conformal projection. It is useful for navigation because straight lines of constant bearing, called rhumb lines, appear straight on the map.

However, areas become increasingly exaggerated towards the poles. Greenland, for example, appears much larger relative to Africa than it really is.

6.8 Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM)

UTM is a projected coordinate system that divides much of the world into 60 zones, each generally 6 degrees of longitude wide. It uses Transverse Mercator projections for these zones.

UTM coordinates are expressed in metres using eastings and northings.

6.9 Factors Considered When Selecting a Projection

  1. Geographical location of the area.
  2. Size and shape of the area.
  3. Orientation of the area.
  4. Purpose of the map.
  5. Property that needs to be preserved.
  6. Required measurement accuracy.
Important examination point: No single map projection preserves all properties of the Earth perfectly. Projection selection depends on the intended purpose of the map.

TOPIC 8: LETTERING AND TYPOGRAPHY IN CARTOGRAPHY

8.1 Meaning of Cartographic Typography

Cartographic typography refers to the selection, arrangement and presentation of written information on maps.

It includes the choice of typeface, font size, weight, colour, spacing and placement of geographical names and other labels.

8.2 Importance of Lettering

  • Identifies geographical features.
  • Helps distinguish different categories of places.
  • Improves map readability.
  • Creates a visual hierarchy.
  • Communicates geographical information clearly.

8.3 Principles of Good Map Lettering

  1. Legibility: Labels must be easy to read.
  2. Consistency: Similar features should be labelled using consistent styles.
  3. Hierarchy: Important features should be given appropriate visual prominence.
  4. Placement: Labels should not hide important map information.
  5. Spacing: Letters should be arranged without unnecessary crowding.
  6. Clarity: Labels should be positioned close to the features they identify.

8.4 Lettering Different Geographical Features

  • Settlements are commonly labelled using appropriate upright text.
  • Rivers may be labelled following the direction or curvature of the river.
  • Mountain ranges may be labelled to follow the general alignment of the range.
  • Large water bodies may use distinctive lettering styles.
  • Administrative regions should have labels that are easy to associate with their boundaries.

8.5 Problems Associated with Poor Lettering

  • Overlapping labels.
  • Small and unreadable text.
  • Inconsistent font sizes.
  • Labels covering important symbols.
  • Confusion between geographical names.
TOPIC 9: MAP DESIGN, LAYOUT AND CARTOGRAPHIC GENERALISATION

9.1 Meaning of Map Design

Map design is the process of planning and arranging geographical information and visual elements to create an effective map.

It involves selecting symbols, colours, typography, scale, projection and layout according to the intended purpose and audience.

9.2 Principles of Good Map Design

1. Visual hierarchy

Important information should attract attention before less important information. For example, the main geographical theme should be more prominent than the source note.

2. Balance

Map elements should be arranged so that the composition appears visually stable and not unnecessarily crowded on one side.

3. Contrast

Different features should be distinguishable through suitable differences in colour, size, shape or line style.

4. Simplicity

A map should communicate its message without unnecessary decorative elements or excessive information.

5. Alignment

Map elements should be arranged in an orderly and consistent manner.

6. Consistency

Similar features should use similar symbols, colours and styles throughout the map.

7. Legibility

All important map information should be easy to read and interpret.

9.3 Map Layout

Map layout refers to the arrangement of the map body, title, legend, scale, north arrow, source and other elements within the available space.

A good layout ensures that the reader can quickly understand the purpose and contents of the map.

9.4 Cartographic Generalisation

Cartographic generalisation is the process of simplifying and modifying geographical information to make it suitable for representation at a particular map scale.

For example, a detailed road network containing hundreds of minor roads may be simplified when preparing a national map.

9.5 Methods of Generalisation

  • Selection: Choosing which geographical features to include.
  • Simplification: Reducing unnecessary detail in lines and boundaries.
  • Aggregation: Combining several small features into one larger representation.
  • Displacement: Moving features slightly to avoid overlap and improve visibility.
  • Classification: Grouping similar features into categories.
  • Exaggeration: Increasing the visual size of a feature so it can be seen clearly.

9.6 Importance of Generalisation

  1. Improves map readability.
  2. Reduces visual overcrowding.
  3. Adapts geographical information to different scales.
  4. Highlights the most important features.
  5. Improves communication and interpretation.
TOPIC 10: RELIEF REPRESENTATION AND CONTOURING

10.1 Meaning of Relief

Relief refers to the differences in elevation and the shape of the Earth's surface. It includes mountains, hills, valleys, plateaus, plains and depressions.

Cartographers use several techniques to represent relief on maps.

10.2 Methods of Representing Relief

A. Contour lines

Contour lines are imaginary lines joining points of equal elevation above or below a chosen reference datum, commonly mean sea level.

B. Spot heights

Spot heights show the measured elevation of specific points on the Earth's surface.

C. Trigonometrical stations

These are surveyed control points whose positions and elevations have been determined through geodetic or surveying methods.

D. Layer colouring

Different colours are used to represent different elevation ranges.

E. Hill shading

Uses simulated illumination and shadows to give a three-dimensional appearance to relief.

F. Hachures

Short lines are used to represent the direction and steepness of slopes.

G. Form lines

Approximate contour-like lines used to indicate general landform shape where exact elevations are not known.

10.3 Contour Characteristics

  1. Each contour line joins points of equal elevation.
  2. Contour lines normally form closed loops, although they may continue beyond the map boundary.
  3. Contour lines generally do not cross one another.
  4. Closely spaced contours indicate steep slopes.
  5. Widely spaced contours indicate gentle slopes.
  6. Evenly spaced contours indicate a relatively uniform slope.
  7. Closed contours with increasing values towards the centre indicate a hill.
  8. Closed contours with decreasing values towards the centre may indicate a depression, depending on the contour convention.

10.4 Contour Interval

Contour interval is the constant vertical difference in elevation between two successive contour lines on a particular map.

Example:

If contours are labelled 100 m, 120 m, 140 m and 160 m, the contour interval is:

120 - 100 = 20 metres.

10.7 Calculating Gradient

Gradient expresses the rate of change in elevation over horizontal distance.

Gradient = Vertical interval ÷ Horizontal distance.

Example:

A hill rises by 200 metres over a horizontal distance of 2 kilometres.

Convert 2 km to 2,000 m.

Gradient = 200 ÷ 2,000

= 1/10.

Answer: 1 in 10.

10.8 Calculating Slope Angle

For a straight slope:

tan θ = Vertical rise ÷ Horizontal distance.

Using the previous example:

tan θ = 200 ÷ 2,000 = 0.1.

θ = tan⁻¹(0.1) ≈ 5.71°.

  • Helps identify mountains, valleys and slopes.
  • Supports route planning.
  • Assists agricultural land evaluation.
  • Supports drainage and watershed studies.
  • Helps engineers select suitable construction sites.
  • Assists environmental and hazard assessment.
TOPIC 11: THEMATIC CARTOGRAPHY AND STATISTICAL MAPS

11.1 Meaning of Thematic Cartography

Thematic cartography is the branch of cartography concerned with representing the spatial distribution, characteristics and relationships of a particular geographical subject.

It uses geographical and statistical information to communicate patterns and variations across space.

11.2 Importance of Thematic Maps

  • Show geographical patterns and differences.
  • Support statistical interpretation.
  • Help identify areas of high or low concentration.
  • Assist planning and policy-making.
  • Communicate research findings.

11.3 Types of Thematic Maps

A. Choropleth maps

Choropleth maps use different shades or colours to represent statistical values within defined geographical areas.

Example: Population density by county in Kenya.

Areas with higher values may be represented by darker shades, while areas with lower values use lighter shades.

Illustrative colour scheme. Actual categories must be determined from the data.

B. Dot distribution maps

Use dots to represent the distribution of a phenomenon. Each dot represents a specified quantity.

Example: One dot represents 1,000 people in a population distribution map.

C. Proportional symbol maps

Use symbols whose sizes vary according to the values being represented.

Example: Circles of different sizes showing population totals in towns.

D. Isopleth maps

Use lines joining points of equal value to represent continuous geographical phenomena.

Examples include isotherms for temperature and isohyets for rainfall.

E. Flow maps

Show the movement of people, goods, information or other phenomena between locations.

Example: Rural-to-urban migration flows.

F. Cartograms

Alter the geographical size or shape of areas according to a selected statistical value.

Example: A world map in which country sizes are adjusted according to population.

G. Dasymetric maps

Use additional geographical information to redistribute statistical data more realistically within larger administrative units.

11.4 Choropleth Mapping and Data Classification

Before preparing a choropleth map, statistical values are grouped into classes.

Common classification methods include:

  • Equal interval: Divides the range of values into intervals of equal width.
  • Quantile: Places approximately equal numbers of geographical units in each class.
  • Natural breaks: Groups similar values while separating relatively distinct groups in the dataset.
  • Standard deviation: Classifies values according to their distance from the mean.

11.5 Important Considerations in Thematic Mapping

  1. Choose a suitable map type.
  2. Use appropriate geographical units.
  3. Understand the nature of the statistical data.
  4. Select suitable classification methods.
  5. Use clear colours and symbols.
  6. Provide a meaningful legend.
  7. Identify the data source and date.
  8. Avoid misleading visual presentation.

11.6 Absolute and Relative Data

Absolute data represent total quantities, such as the number of people or total crop production.

Relative data express quantities in relation to another measure, such as population density, percentage or rate.

Choropleth maps generally work best with standardised data such as density, percentages and rates rather than raw totals.

TOPIC 13: MAP READING, INTERPRETATION AND ANALYSIS

13.1 Meaning of Map Reading

Map reading is the process of examining a map to understand the geographical information it contains.

13.2 Meaning of Map Interpretation

Map interpretation involves explaining the meaning, patterns, relationships and geographical processes represented by the features on a map.

13.3 Steps in Map Reading

  1. Read the title.
  2. Examine the scale.
  3. Study the legend and symbols.
  4. Identify the orientation and coordinates.
  5. Locate the geographical features.
  6. Examine the spatial distribution of features.
  7. Identify patterns and relationships.
  8. Draw geographical conclusions supported by the map.

13.4 Relief Interpretation

Relief interpretation involves identifying landforms and slope characteristics using contours, spot heights and other elevation information.

Examples:

  • Closely spaced contours indicate steep slopes.
  • Widely spaced contours indicate gentle slopes.
  • Concentric closed contours with increasing elevation towards the centre indicate a hill.
  • V-shaped contours can help identify valleys and drainage patterns.

13.5 Drainage Pattern Interpretation

Drainage patterns refer to the arrangement of rivers and streams within a drainage basin.

  • Dendritic: Tree-like branching pattern, common where underlying materials have relatively uniform resistance.
  • Trellis: Main streams and tributaries arranged in a roughly parallel and right-angle pattern, often associated with folded or alternating resistant and weak rocks.
  • Radial: Streams flow outward from a central elevated feature, such as a volcanic mountain.
  • Centripetal: Streams flow towards a central depression or basin.
  • Rectangular: Streams follow a pattern controlled by joints and faults.

13.6 Settlement Pattern Interpretation

Settlements may be described according to their spatial arrangement:

  • Linear: Buildings are arranged along roads, rivers or other transport routes.
  • Nucleated: Buildings are concentrated around a central point.
  • Dispersed: Buildings are spread over a relatively large area.

13.7 Map Analysis

Map analysis involves using geographical information to investigate spatial relationships, patterns and changes.

Examples include:

  • Comparing settlement distribution with road networks.
  • Examining the relationship between relief and agricultural activities.
  • Identifying areas with limited access to public facilities.
  • Comparing land use patterns across different periods.

TOPIC 15: APPLICATIONS OF CARTOGRAPHY IN KENYA

15.1 Introduction

Cartography is important in Kenya because it supports national development, land administration, infrastructure development, environmental management and geographical research.

15.2 Land Administration

Cadastral maps are used to represent land parcels, boundaries and property-related information. They support land registration, planning and administration.

15.3 Urban and Regional Planning

Maps are used to show settlement growth, land use, road networks, public facilities and urban expansion in towns such as Nairobi, Kisumu, Mombasa and Nakuru.

15.4 Environmental Management

Thematic maps help monitor forests, wetlands, rivers, biodiversity and environmental degradation.

Examples include mapping forest cover in the Mau Forest Complex and wetlands around Lake Victoria.

15.5 Agricultural Planning

Maps showing soil types, rainfall, slope and land use help identify suitable agricultural areas and support agricultural planning.

15.6 Transport and Infrastructure

Road maps, railway maps and infrastructure maps support route planning, transport management and the location of development projects.

15.7 Disaster Management

Flood-risk maps, drought maps and landslide susceptibility maps help authorities identify vulnerable areas and prepare appropriate interventions.

15.8 Population and Social Planning

Population maps help explain population distribution, density, settlement patterns and the spatial accessibility of public services.

15.9 Cartography and Sustainable Development

Cartographic information supports sustainable development by helping decision-makers identify resource distribution, environmental problems, population needs and suitable areas for development.

TOPIC 17: REVISION QUESTIONS AND UNIVERSITY EXAMINATION ANSWERS

SECTION A: SHORT-ANSWER QUESTIONS

1. Define cartography.

Answer: Cartography is the science, art and technology of making, designing, producing and interpreting maps.

2. What is a map?

Answer: A map is a scaled, simplified and symbolic representation of selected geographical features or phenomena on the Earth's surface.

3. Differentiate between large-scale and small-scale maps.

Answer: Large-scale maps have smaller scale denominators and show greater detail over smaller areas. Small-scale maps have larger denominators and show less detail over larger areas.

4. State four elements of a map.

Answer: Title, legend, scale and north arrow. Other acceptable answers include source, date, grid and author.

5. Define map projection.

Answer: A map projection is a mathematical method of transforming the curved surface of the Earth onto a flat map.

6. What is a contour line?

Answer: A contour line is an imaginary line joining points of equal elevation above or below a selected reference datum.

7. What is a thematic map?

Answer: A thematic map is a map designed to represent the distribution or characteristics of a specific geographical subject.

8. Define cartographic generalisation.

Answer: It is the process of simplifying and modifying geographical information to make it suitable for representation at a particular map scale.

9. What is a map legend?

Answer: A map legend is a key that explains the meaning of the symbols, colours and patterns used on a map.

10. State four types of map projections.

Answer: Mercator, Transverse Mercator, Lambert Conformal Conic and Albers Equal-Area Conic. Other valid projections include Robinson and Azimuthal Equidistant.

SECTION B: CALCULATION QUESTIONS

11. A map has a scale of 1:50,000. Calculate the ground distance represented by 6 cm.

Solution:

Ground distance = 6 × 50,000 = 300,000 cm.

= 3,000 metres.

Answer: 3 km.

12. Two towns are 10 km apart on the ground. Their distance on a map is 5 cm. Calculate the RF.

Solution:

10 km = 1,000,000 cm.

RF = 5 : 1,000,000.

Divide both sides by 5.

Answer: 1:200,000.

13. Calculate the ground distance if 3.5 cm is measured on a map with a scale of 1:100,000.

Solution:

3.5 × 100,000 = 350,000 cm.

= 3,500 metres.

Answer: 3.5 km.

14. A hill rises 150 metres over a horizontal distance of 1,500 metres. Calculate its gradient.

Solution:

Gradient = 150 ÷ 1,500.

= 1/10.

Answer: 1 in 10.

15. A topographic map has contours of 200 m, 250 m, 300 m and 350 m. Determine the contour interval.

Solution:

250 - 200 = 50 m.

Answer: 50 metres.
